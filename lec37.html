Lecture 37 MED priority

Green Cloud

Prof. Soumya Kanti Ghosh · Dept. of Computer Science and Engineering, IIT Kharagpur

Examines the energy consumption problem of cloud data centers — why it matters, how data centers are structured, and how energy is distributed across computing vs cooling infrastructure. Introduces green cloud computing: carbon-aware brokering, green middleware, and the PUE metric. Awareness-level content; statistics are from 2007-era reports but the framework and metrics remain relevant.

1 · The Energy Problem in Cloud
Dark side of cloud: Large-scale cloud infrastructure consumes enormous amounts of energy. The cost of the computing equipment in a data center is roughly matched by the cost of environmental infrastructure (space, cooling, power) — and in some locations, environmental costs exceed hardware costs.

Why Energy is a Scaling Constraint

Supply Constraint
Energy Supply Limits Growth
Beyond a certain scale, even if a provider has capital to build more data centers, the available power supply in a region becomes a binding constraint. Cloud growth is increasingly limited by energy infrastructure, not hardware.
Carbon Footprint
Environmental Impact
More energy consumption → more electricity generation → higher carbon dioxide emissions. Gartner (2007) estimated the IT industry contributes 2% of total global CO₂ emissions. Data center emissions worldwide now exceed combined emissions of Argentina and the Netherlands.
Operational Cost
Energy as % of OpEx
Energy accounts for approximately 10% of data center operational cost — and projected to rise to 50% within a few years (as of the report era). Amazon estimated energy-related costs at 42% of total data center budget (including cooling amortized over 15 years).
Waste
Idle Server Consumption
An idle server still consumes approximately 66% of its peak CPU load energy. Even when serving no requests, keeping servers on to maintain availability imposes a significant baseline energy cost — analogous to an ice cream parlor running refrigeration with no customers.
Consolidation insight from lecture: If 4 servers each have 2 VMs running (spread for load distribution), each server runs at low utilization but all 4 draw energy. Better: consolidate 8 VMs onto 2 servers fully loaded, and put the other 2 into sleep/passive mode. Same compute served, ~50% fewer active servers, significant energy savings — but SLA constraints and scheduling complexity make this non-trivial.
2 · Data Center Energy Distribution

Worth watching: Pie/bar chart showing the split of energy consumption — computing 40%, cooling 45%, power distribution 15%. Key takeaway: the majority of energy goes to non-computing infrastructure.
ComponentShare of Total EnergyNotes
Cooling System~45%Largest single consumer; cooling system cost alone: $2–5M/year (per report-era figures)
Computing Equipment~40%Servers, storage — the actual compute work only uses 40% of total power drawn
Power Distribution~15%UPS, power conversion losses, wiring infrastructure
Key implication: 60% of data center energy is consumed by infrastructure supporting the compute, not by compute itself. Green cloud efforts must target cooling efficiency and power distribution — not just server utilization.

Server Energy Model

A server's power draw has two components:

Fixed Power (Idle State)
What draws itMemory modules, disk, always-on circuitry
Magnitude~66% of peak CPU load even at idle
ImplicationTurning off idle servers saves the remaining 34% — not the full power cost
Variable Power (Under Load)
What draws itCPU frequency scaling, active computation
MagnitudeUp to 100% of peak load
ImplicationConsolidating workloads onto fewer servers raises per-server utilization and reduces total active-server count

Switch/Network Energy Model

For chassis-based blade switches (typical in data centers):

ComponentShare of Switch Power
Chassis~36%
Line cards~43%
Port transceivers~11%
Model: P_switch = P_chassis + (n_linecards × P_linecard) + Σ(n_ports × P_transceiver). Network infrastructure energy is significant and must be included in any green cloud energy model.

4 · Green Cloud Computing — Definition
Green computing: Environmentally responsible and eco-friendly use of computers and their resources. Encompasses the design, manufacturing, engineering, use, and disposal of computing devices in ways that minimize environmental impact.
Green cloud computing: Achieves not only efficient processing and utilization of computing infrastructure, but also minimizes energy consumption — both goals simultaneously. Performance + energy efficiency, not performance at the cost of energy.

Efficiency at Every Layer

IaaS Layer Most visible energy impact: server consolidation, VM placement, cooling, power management — primary focus of green cloud research
PaaS Layer Green profiler, power capping, green compiler, green resource allocation — reducing energy consumed by platforms and middleware
SaaS / Application Layer Algorithm efficiency matters — a more efficient algorithm with lower CPU complexity reduces energy consumption that propagates down through all layers
Key insight: Energy optimization is not only an IaaS problem. An inefficient algorithm at the SaaS layer that takes unnecessary CPU cycles increases energy consumption at every layer below it. Green cloud must address the full stack.
5 · Performance vs Energy Efficiency
The core tension: Traditional resource management optimizes for performance and SLA compliance. Green cloud adds energy efficiency as a co-equal objective — the scheduler must balance both, not just one.
Performance Goal
Maximize Service Quality
Minimize response time, maintain SLA, avoid service violations, ensure high availability. This typically means keeping more servers active and spreading load.
Energy Goal
Minimize Energy Consumption
Consolidate VMs onto fewer servers, power down idle servers, route traffic through fewer network paths. Conflicts with performance spread — requires careful scheduling.
Provider Pressure
Protect Profit Margin
Energy costs at Amazon: ~42% of total data center budget. As costs rise, providers must reduce energy per unit of compute to maintain profitability — green computing is economically motivated, not just altruistic.
Location Strategy
Data Centers Near Power Sources
Google, Microsoft, Yahoo build data centers near cheap hydroelectric power (e.g., Columbia River region) to exploit lower-cost electricity and reduce transmission losses.

Green Broker

Green Broker: An extension of the standard cloud broker that adds carbon-aware scheduling. In addition to analyzing user requirements, calculating cost, and scheduling services, the green broker calculates the carbon footprint of each provisioning decision and selects the option that minimizes carbon emissions while meeting SLA.

Green Broker Components

Existing
Grid Negotiator / Service Analyzer / Consumer Profiler
Standard broker components — negotiate resources, analyze what services the consumer needs, and profile usage patterns.
New: Green Addition
Energy Monitor + Carbon-Aware Scheduler
Energy monitor tracks real-time power consumption of VMs and servers. Carbon-aware scheduler factors CO₂ impact into service scheduling decisions — prioritizes lower-carbon CSPs when performance requirements allow.

7 · Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE)
PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness): A metric to measure the energy efficiency of a data center.

PUE = Total Facility Power / IT Equipment Power

A PUE of 1.0 would mean 100% of power goes to IT equipment with zero overhead — physically impossible. A PUE of 2.0 means for every watt of compute, another watt is spent on cooling, lighting, and distribution. Industry best-in-class is ~1.1–1.2 (large hyperscale providers).
Research status: PUE measurement methodology and what counts as "IT equipment power" vs "facility power" is still debated. Green cloud computing has significant open research questions in energy modeling, carbon-aware scheduling, and cross-layer optimization.
Summary
Energy Breakdown: ~45% cooling, ~40% computing, ~15% power distribution. The majority of a data center's energy is consumed by support infrastructure, not the compute itself — so cooling optimization is as important as server consolidation.
Idle Server Problem: An idle server still draws ~66% of peak power. VM consolidation (packing VMs onto fewer active servers and sleeping the rest) is the primary lever for energy savings, but creates tension with SLA and load distribution.
Green Broker: Adds carbon footprint calculation and carbon-aware scheduling to the standard cloud brokering function — selecting CSPs and VMs based on both QoS and energy/carbon cost.
PUE: Power Usage Effectiveness = Total Facility Power / IT Equipment Power. PUE = 1.0 is ideal; industry best-in-class approaches 1.1. Lowering PUE is the primary data center efficiency target.
Economics motivate green: Energy is 42%+ of Amazon's data center budget. Green computing is not purely environmental — it is a cost imperative for large-scale cloud providers whose profitability depends on energy efficiency at scale.
