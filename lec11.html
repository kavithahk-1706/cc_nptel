Lecture 11 High priority

Service Level Agreement (SLA)

Prof. Soumya Kanti Ghosh · Dept. of Computer Science and Engineering, IIT Kharagpur

Covers the service level agreement, the formal contract between cloud provider and consumer: its contents, SLOs and KPIs, web service SLA versus cloud SLA, types of SLA, considerations and requirements, monitoring metrics, and example SLAs of real cloud providers. Concept-heavy and applied; the SLA-SLO-KPI hierarchy and the list of requirements are likely exam material.

1 · What is an SLA?
Whenever a service provider and a consumer exchange metered services involving costing, there must be an agreement on pricing, service availability and other quality factors. A consumer moving from their own proprietary computing to a cloud expects the same reliability, performance and security they had on their own premises, where the system was in their control. Today there is no very standard format for this; usually one signs off the provider's terms and conditions.
Service Level Agreement (SLA): a formal contract between a service provider (SP) and a service consumer (SC). It is the foundation of consumer trust in the provider and a basis for comparing one provider with another. Its purpose is to define a formal basis for the performance and availability the provider guarantees to deliver (e.g. a guaranteed uptime of 95 percent). The SLA and SLOs are the basis of selecting a cloud provider.

Service Level Objectives (SLOs)

An SLA contains SLOs: objectively measurable conditions for the service. They cannot be verbal; they must be measurable, e.g. "performance should be 95 percent". Objectives vary between consumers and over time:
Examples: an academic institution may accept 95 percent uptime but need much higher data persistence. Another organization may need uptime above 99 percent but not require backup. Availability may need to exceed 99 percent during peak hours but only 90 percent during off-peak hours.
2 · SLA Contents
SLAs apply to IaaS, PaaS, SaaS and other services such as data services, so an SLA corresponds to the particular service model. A typical SLA contains:
services
Set of services and their definitions
The set of services the provider will deliver, with a complete, specific definition of each service.
duties
Responsibilities
The responsibilities of the provider and the consumer.
measurement
Metrics
A set of metrics to measure whether the provider is offering the services as guaranteed.
monitoring
Auditing mechanism
An auditing mechanism to monitor the services, possibly by a third party auditor who checks for violations.
violations
Remedies and penalties
Remedies available to the consumer and the provider if terms are not satisfied (payback or other incentive), like penalties in an MoU.
time
Change over time
How the SLA will change over time (within a day or over different days).
3 · Web Service SLA and Cloud SLA
Cloud evolved from web services and SOA, so cloud SLAs are related to web service SLAs.

TemplateSpecifies the nature of the agreement template (the agreed format)
DiscoveryFacilitates discovering compatible providers
InteractionUsually request-response
ViolationDynamically managed and verified
WSLA (Web Service Level Agreement framework)
WhatA formal XML-schema based language to express SLAs, plus a runtime interpreter

GapLack of formal definitions for the semantics of the metrics (e.g. whether a parameter means the system is going down or a failure is expected)

Cloud SLA vs web service SLA

AspectTraditional web serviceCloud computing
QoS parametersResponse time, SLA violation rate for reliability, availability, cost of servicesQoS related to security, privacy, trust, management etc. is more important
AutomationSLA negotiation, provisioning, service delivery and monitoring are not automatedSLA automation is required for highly dynamic and scalable service consumption (scale up and down)
Resource allocationUDDI registry is used for advertising and discovering between web servicesResources are allocated and distributed globally without any central directory
4 · Types of SLA
Off-the-shelf (non-negotiable / direct) SLA
SuitabilityNot conducive for mission-critical data or applications
CreationThe provider creates the SLA template and defines all criteria (contract period, billing, response time, availability etc.)
UseFollowed by present day state-of-the-art clouds, especially public clouds, where you accept the terms and conditions
Negotiable SLA
NegotiationVia an external agent between provider and consumer, or via multiple external agents (e.g. when several services are combined)
UseSuited to large requirements, such as moving a whole organization's process to the cloud
Analogy: booking one or two rooms or seats, you take whatever is available (off-the-shelf); booking a whole hotel or a whole bus, you negotiate a special rate and fine tune the agreement.
Rule of thumb: small requirement or small scale operation gives off-the-shelf; large requirement gives negotiable.
5 · SLO Examples and Service Level Management
An SLO encompasses multiple QoS parameters, such as availability, serviceability, billing, penalties, throughput, response time or quality.
Availability of service X is 99.9% Response time of a database query Q is between 3 to 5 seconds Throughput of a server S at peak load time is 0.875
Service level management: monitoring and measuring the performance of services based on SLOs. From the provider perspective, decisions are based on business objectives and technical realities (how much the provider has at the back end). From the consumer perspective, decisions are about how to use cloud services and whether they suit the organization or personal need.
6 · Considerations for SLA
ConsiderationMeaning
Business level objectivesConsumers should know why they are using cloud services before deciding how to use them
Responsibilities of provider and consumerThe balance varies by service type. Example: if the provider accepts data at x megabits per second and the consumer sends more, overflow may violate the provider's other SLAs; both must check, and auditing maintains this
Business continuity and disaster recoveryConsumers should ensure the provider has adequate protection in case of disaster (natural, man-made, system failure)
System redundancyMany providers deliver services via massively redundant systems so consumers do not experience outages even if drives, network connections or servers fail
MaintenanceMaintenance of the cloud infrastructure affects any cloud offering (software and hardware)
Location of dataIf the provider promises to enforce data location regulations, the consumer must be able to audit it to prove compliance (e.g. data staying within India)
Seizure of dataIf law enforcement targets one consumer's data, multi-tenancy makes it likely that other consumers are affected, so consider keeping backups with a third party
Failure of the providerConsider the financial health of the provider, contingency plans, and its policies for handling data of a delinquent or disputed account
JurisdictionUnderstand the laws that apply to any cloud provider considered, and where litigation would be addressed
7 · SLA Requirements
RequirementMeaning
SecurityThe consumer must understand the controls and federation patterns needed to meet security requirements; providers must understand what to deliver to enable them
Data encryptionDetails of encryption and access control policies, including key management (where the encrypted data and keys reside and how keys are communicated)
PrivacyIsolation of customer data (and services) in a multi-tenant environment
Data retention and deletionProviders may have legal requirements to retain data even if the consumer deleted it; they must prove compliance with these policies (how long data is retained, where deleted)
Hardware erasure and destructionThe provider must zero out memory when a consumer powers off a VM, and even the disk platters if hardware is disposed of or recycled
Regulatory complianceIf regulations apply to data and applications, providers must be able to prove compliance
TransparencyFor critical data and applications, providers must proactively notify consumers when SLA terms are breached
CertificationThe provider should be responsible for proving the certification of any data or applications and keeping it up to date
MonitoringTo eliminate conflict of interest, a neutral third party organization is the best solution; easy to say but hard to implement, since a provider may not let a third party work on its systems
AuditabilityConsumers are liable for breaches, so they should be able to audit provider systems and procedures; the SLA should state how and when audits take place. Audits are disruptive and expensive (downtime, resources), so providers will place limits on them and charge for them

8 · KPIs, SLOs and SLA
▶ 24:51
Worth watching: the hand-drawn hierarchy with KPI at the bottom leading up to SLO and then SLA.
SLA Aggregation of SLOs
SLO Built from KPIs
KPI Lowest level metric
Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) are low-level resource metrics measured directly from system parameters, such as downtime, uptime, in-bytes, out-bytes and packet size. Multiple KPIs are composed, aggregated or converted into high-level SLOs, and multiple SLOs are integrated into the SLA.
Possible mapping: availability (A) = 1 - (downtime / uptime), as written on the slide.

Industry-defined KPIs: monitoring and auditability

Natural questions: who should monitor the performance of the provider, and does the consumer meet its responsibilities? The solution is a neutral third-party organization, which eliminates conflicts of interest (e.g. when the provider reports an outage at its sole discretion, or the consumer is responsible for the outage). For auditability, the consumer needs to know whether the provider adheres to legal regulations or industry standards, and the SLA should make clear how and when audits are conducted.
9 · Metrics for Monitoring and Auditing
MetricMeaning
ThroughputHow quickly the service responds
AvailabilityPercentage of uptime for a service in a given observation period
ReliabilityHow often the service is available
Load balancingWhen elasticity kicks in (new VMs booted or terminated), how well load is balanced
DurabilityHow likely the data is to be lost
ElasticityAbility of a resource to grow infinitely, with limits (e.g. maximum storage or bandwidth) clearly stated
LinearityHow a system performs as the load increases
AgilityHow quickly the provider responds as the consumer's resource load scales up and down
AutomationPercentage of requests handled without any human interaction
Customer service response timesHow quickly the provider responds to a service request (human interaction when something goes wrong with on-demand, self-service)
Service-level violation rateMean rate of SLA violation due to infringements of the agreed warranty levels
Transaction timeTime from when a service is invoked until the transaction completes, including delays
Resolution timeTime between detection of a service problem and its resolution
Linearity: if provisioning increases linearly as load increases, it is easy to scale up and chase higher demand; if it is non-linear (especially exponential), scaling is difficult.

Summary
SLA: a formal contract between provider and consumer, the foundation of consumer trust, containing objectively measurable SLOs; there is no standard format yet.
SLA contents: services and their definitions, responsibilities, metrics, an auditing mechanism, remedies and penalties, and how the SLA changes over time.
Hierarchy: KPIs (low-level system metrics) are aggregated into SLOs, which together make up the SLA; for example availability = 1 - (downtime / uptime).
Cloud vs web service SLA: cloud stresses security, privacy and trust, needs automated SLAs for dynamic scaling, and allocates resources globally with no central directory (unlike UDDI).
Types: off-the-shelf (non-negotiable, provider template, public clouds, small needs) versus negotiable (via external agents, large needs).
Caveats: third party monitoring is hard to implement, audits are costly so providers limit them, and multi-tenancy means data seizure for one consumer can affect others.
Requirements: security, encryption, privacy, retention and deletion, hardware erasure, compliance, transparency, certification, monitoring and auditability; they differ across IaaS, PaaS and SaaS, and only IaaS needs machine-readable SLAs in the slide's matrix.
Examples: EC2 and Azure Compute 99.95%; S3, Azure Storage, Google App Engine and Nirvanix 99.9%; Terremark 100% monthly uptime; Salesforce CRM and SimpleDB offer no SLA guarantees.
