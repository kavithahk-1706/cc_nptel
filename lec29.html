Lecture 29 MED priority

Cloud Security IV — Collaborative SaaS Cloud Security

Prof. Soumya Kanti Ghosh · Dept. of Computer Science and Engineering, IIT Kharagpur

Covers security challenges when SaaS clouds collaborate in a loosely coupled federation. Topics include selecting a trustworthy cloud provider (SelCSP framework: trust, reputation, competence), risk-based access control, inter-domain role mapping (IDRM), and detection/removal of cyclic inheritance and separation-of-duty conflicts. Research-level content; useful for understanding multi-cloud security architecture.

1 · Collaborative SaaS Cloud — The Problem
Collaborative SaaS cloud: Multiple cloud-hosted applications owned by different organizations communicate and share data with each other — loosely coupled federation across SaaS instances, domains, or providers.
Tightly coupled
Strong connectivity, full information
Federating clouds with strong bindings. Existing security mechanisms are mostly designed for tightly coupled systems — full credentials and policies are available to all parties.
Loosely coupled
Dynamic, partial information
Instances in different clouds collaborate with minimal shared context. Parties may not carry all credentials or know all security settings of their collaborators. This is the more common and harder-to-secure scenario.

Cloud-specific security challenges in this model

Co-tenancy — shared physical infrastructure Lack of control over outsourced data and apps Inadequate policies and SLA non-standardization Integrity of data shared across multiple users No audit trails directly available to the consumer Choosing an ideal SaaS cloud provider
SaaS has maximum provider control: Security responsibility shifts almost entirely to the provider; the consumer controls only their data and its usage — not the application platform or underlying infrastructure.
2 · SelCSP — Selecting a Trustworthy SaaS Provider
SelCSP framework: Given a set of registered Cloud Service Providers (CSPs), recommend the best-suited CSP for a customer's business outsourcing requirement, evaluated on three axes: Trust, Reputation, and Competence.
Trust
How much the customer can rely on the provider's honesty, data privacy, and service integrity. Estimated from interaction history and third-party assessments.
Reputation
Community-based rating from past interactions. Computed from temporal interaction matrices and ratings. Distinct from trust — a provider can have high reputation but lower trust for a specific task type.
Competence
Whether the provider has the technical capability and SLA guarantees to actually deliver the required service. Standard SLAs are non-uniform across providers — risk estimation accounts for this.
SLA limitation: Most cloud SLAs guarantee availability but not other business-critical performance assurances. There is no standard set of SLA clauses across providers — compensation and penalty structures are non-uniform.
3 · Risk-Based Access Control for Anonymous Users
Problem: Anonymous users (from collaborating domains) request access to local resources. They may lack the exact permissions required. A binary allow/deny decision either blocks legitimate collaboration or creates over-exposure.
Traditional MLS (binary)
DecisionAllow or deny based on exact permission match
Risk treatmentDiscards many requests that would have been safe
FlexibilityLow — reduces collaboration to eliminate risk
Risk-based access control
DecisionGrant access even without exact permissions, up to a risk threshold
Risk treatmentBalance access risk against security uncertainty; quantify operational need
FlexibilityHigh — maximizes information sharing while keeping risk low
Fuzzy inference system: Used to quantify requester reputation and local object security level, then compute authorized permissions. Analogous to physical access: a visitor can enter the lobby and the visitor lounge, but not the labs — the permitted subset depends on reputation and need.
4 · IDRM — Inter-Domain Role Mapping
IDRM problem: When a user from Domain A (with role R_A) wants to access resources in Domain B, their role must be mapped to an equivalent role in B's role hierarchy. The goal is to find a minimal set of Domain B roles that encompasses exactly the requested permissions — no more.
IDRM Availability
GoalMaximize the permissions that can be granted from available roles
ObjectiveMinimize the number of extra (excess) permissions granted
IDRM Safety
GoalEnsure no unauthorized permissions are granted under any mapping
ObjectiveEnsure the mapped roles do not exceed the requesting role's intended permissions
Complexity: IDRM has no polynomial-time exact solution. Greedy search-based heuristics provide sub-optimal but practically useful solutions. Multiple minimal role sets may exist; no single role set may exactly match all requested permissions.
Principle of minimal access: Grant only the permissions necessary to execute the requested task. If read-only access suffices, do not grant read-write. Role mapping must respect this constraint across domain boundaries.
5 · Access Conflict Detection & Removal
Problem: In loosely coupled multi-domain collaboration, role inheritance chains can create cyclic paths that grant access the requester was never supposed to have.

Two types of conflict

Cyclic inheritance conflict
Role cycle enables unauthorized access
Subject S is not allowed to write to object O directly, but inherits a role that inherits a role that inherits write permission on O. The cycle allows what should be blocked. Detection scans for unsafe role cycles in the collaboration graph.
SoD constraint violation
Separation of Duty broken via inter-domain path
A bank teller who issues a draft cannot also verify it (SoD). In multi-domain collaboration, a cross-domain role chain may allow the same entity to hold both roles via intermediate paths — violating SoD even though each domain's local policy is correct.

Conflict removal strategy

Collaborating role (virtual role): When no exact role match exists that resolves the conflict, create a synthetic "collaborating role" that grants only the safe subset of permissions. This virtual role breaks the unsafe cycle and prevents the conflict from re-emerging through other inheritance paths.
Two cases: (1) Exact match role exists — use it directly with modified inheritance links. (2) No exact match — create a sub-role or dummy role that includes only the necessary permissions, inserted into the hierarchy to break the problematic cycle.
6 · Framework Summary
StageComponentProblem solved
1SelCSPSelect a trustworthy and competent SaaS cloud provider before outsourcing, using trust + reputation + competence scoring
2Risk-based access controlAuthorize anonymous collaborating users with incomplete credentials by balancing access risk and security uncertainty via fuzzy inference
3IDRMMap authorized permissions to local roles in the target domain with minimal excess permission (greedy heuristic)
4Conflict detection & removalDetect cyclic inheritance conflicts and SoD violations; resolve by creating collaborating (virtual) roles that break unsafe cycles
Summary
Loosely coupled collaboration is the hard case: Parties have incomplete credentials and unknown security settings of their collaborators. Tight coupling security mechanisms do not transfer directly.
SelCSP: Evaluate providers on Trust + Reputation + Competence before outsourcing; standard SLAs alone are insufficient because they lack standard clauses and compensation structures.
IDRM: Mapping cross-domain roles is NP-hard in general; greedy heuristics find minimal-excess-permission role sets. Two variants — IDRM availability (maximize grantable permissions) and IDRM safety (prevent over-grant).
SoD violations in federation: Even when each domain's local policy is correct, inter-domain role chains can create cycles that violate Separation of Duty. Detection + collaborating role creation is the mitigation strategy.
