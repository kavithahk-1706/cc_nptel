Lecture 04 High priority

Cloud Computing Architecture

Prof. Soumya Kanti Ghosh · Dept. of Computer Science and Engineering, IIT Kharagpur

Introduces cloud architecture: the path from business goals to quality attributes to architectural tactics, the XaaS stack with its cross-layer management and security, scale up vs scale out for elasticity, and the XaaS family of service models including business process as a service. Concept-heavy and foundational for the architecture lectures that follow.

1 · High-Level Architectural Approach
From the cloud perspective the aim is to achieve some business goal (for consumers, for the producer, or for the overall cloud framework). This needs quality attributes to be managed, which in turn are achieved through architectural tactics.
Business goals TCO, quality, market share, flexibility, stakeholder satisfaction, compliance
Quality attributes Availability, elasticity, interoperability, security, adaptability, performance, usability, maintainability
Architectural tactics Stateless design, loose coupling, caching, claim-based authentication, scale-out architecture, pipelining, divide and conquer, firewall traversal, partitioning, publish-subscribe, strong encryption, multi-tenancy, reliable messaging, asynchronous communication
Business goals (from the cloud service provider's point of view): TCO (total cost to the organization), stakeholder satisfaction, compliance with standards, market share and flexibility.
Quality attributes are the major characteristics of cloud seen in earlier lectures (how available, how elastic, how interoperable). They let a service provider define their business goal across the different cloud markets.
Architectural tactics are the architectural techniques used to achieve these: e.g. stateless design, loose coupling (devices are geographically spread and heterogeneous), interconnection and homogeneity over broad network access, caching, and claim-based authentication.
Note: these aspects manifest differently in each service model (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS), so keep this in mind while developing or proposing any architectural view.

3 · Cloud Architecture — the XaaS Stack
Client infrastructure Interacts with the application
Application (SaaS) Software as a Service
Service and cloud runtime (PaaS) Middleware or platform: services, cloud runtime libraries
Storage and infrastructure (IaaS) Infrastructure as a Service
Two vertical stacks, Management and Security, span all the layers. Some references also include quality of service (QoS) alongside management. Security or management cannot be achieved by considering a single layer, so cross-layer consideration is needed, which is why they are vertical stacks.
Responsibility depends on the service model: with IaaS, once the infrastructure (e.g. virtual machines) is provisioned, management and security of what is run on it is the consumer's responsibility. With SaaS (e.g. word processing or CRM), management and security are provided by the provider up to that layer.

4 · Architecture for Elasticity
Elasticity (scaling up and down) is one of the most important characteristics of cloud. There are two broad approaches, which also apply to elastic computing without cloud.
Vertical scale up
IdeaAdd more resources to a single computation unit, i.e. buy a bigger box
WorkloadMove the workload to a computation unit with more resources
Best forSmall scenarios: probably cheaper, and code "just works"
Horizontal scale out
IdeaAdd more computation units and have them act in concert
WorkloadSplit the workload across multiple units; database partitioning
Best forLarger scenarios: scale out is the only solution, and a 1×64-way server is much more expensive than 64×1-way servers
Scale-up example: a rack that starts with 24 servers gets more servers added; or a chassis holding up to 16 blades starts with 4 and is later filled to 16.
Caveat: expanding a large scenario by scaling up is costly, and also affects maintainability and failure rate. A single system is a single point of failure; with multiple systems, if some fail the rest can continue at a lower performance level. The choice depends on requirements.
5 · Service Models (XaaS)
XaaS (anything/everything as a service): a generalization for cloud-related services. The combination of service-oriented infrastructure (SOI) and cloud computing realizes XaaS. It refers to an increasing number of services delivered over the Internet rather than provided locally or on-site, and is the essence of cloud computing.
XaaS is a service driven approach to infrastructure, platform or any other type of offering. Service oriented architecture is to be revisited in subsequent lectures.

XaaS layers

BPaaS — Business Process as a Service Horizontal or vertical business processes provided on a subscription basis to companies or industries
SaaS — Software as a Service Software applications hosted in the cloud and provided on a subscription basis
PaaS — Platform as a Service Virtualized application development and run-time platform
IaaS — Infrastructure as a Service Raw infrastructure (CPU, memory, storage, network) available on an as-needed basis

Business Process as a Service (BPaaS)

A business workflow offered as a service. It is not a single application: different processes or applications are orchestrated, and an application depends on other applications. It is an integration of sub-processes or sub-applications with a timing relationship, described by a process execution tree or graph. Examples: banking sector, development planning operations.

Why it matters: it is more than SaaS. The processes may come from different, heterogeneous providers, so the user collaborates with different cloud service providers. This matters to organizations; a popular language for defining business process flow is the business process execution language.

Examples given: database as a service means no need to install a database, just use it as a service. Monitoring/management as a service: managing a large IT infrastructure such as the institute's labs, from software issues to hardware problems, through a service connected to the machines.
6 · Requirements of a Cloud Service Provider (CSP)
Increase productivity Increase end user satisfaction Increase innovation Increase agility
The provider seeks these to hold its market position, and to offer its infrastructure as an asset that can configure itself to need.
XaaS fulfils all four demands: broad network access (cloud) + resource pooling (cloud) + business-driven infrastructure on-demand (SOI) + service orientation (SOI) = XaaS.
7 · Classical Service Model vs XaaS

Classical service model (layers managed by the user)

Applications Top of the stack
Data Closely tied to the application
Runtime Runtime libraries
Middleware Middleware layer
O/S Operating system placed on the virtual machine
Virtualization Virtual machines, virtual network, virtual storage
Servers Physical infrastructure
Storage Omnipresent; kept as a separate aspect because storage management is a different matter
Network Underlying broad network access that lets things talk to each other
This is a logical representation, not a strict layering where one thing is always done over the other. Network, storage and servers are the bare-metal building blocks. Not all components are equally important for every XaaS type, but each plays a role.
Characteristics of the classical model: all layers (hardware, OS, development tools, applications) are managed by the users; initial IT budget and resources are required; users bear the cost of hardware, maintenance and technology; each system is designed and funded for a specific business activity (custom build-to-order); systems are deployed as a tightly coupled vertical stack so no single part can easily be replaced or changed; manual operations for provisioning and management prevail. Result: legacy IT.

8 · Client-Server Architecture
A server serves several clients over the network. The server program runs on the server and waits for clients to connect. This classical architecture is extremely popular and still in use in many cases; examples are FTP server, telnet and HTTP, where a daemon on the server side talks to the client.
Migration to SOA: instead of the strongly coupled client-server model, services talk to each other. The distinct advantages are that heterogeneous things can talk to each other and there is much more flexibility.
Summary
Architectural approach: business goals (TCO, compliance, market share, flexibility) are achieved through quality attributes (availability, elasticity, security and so on), which are achieved through architectural tactics.
Building blocks: technical architecture (XaaS stack, middleware, management and security) and deployment operation architecture (geo-location check, operation and monitoring).
XaaS stack: IaaS at the bottom, PaaS in the middle, SaaS on top, with vertical management and security spanning every layer; the consumer's responsibility for them depends on the service model.
Elasticity: scale up adds resources to one box and suits small scenarios; scale out adds many units and suits large scenarios and avoids a single point of failure.
XaaS: the combination of service-oriented infrastructure and cloud computing; SaaS, PaaS, IaaS, BPaaS and many other services delivered over the Internet.
BPaaS: orchestrated business workflows offered as a service, possibly across heterogeneous providers.
CSP needs: productivity, end user satisfaction, innovation and agility, all fulfilled by XaaS.
Legacy IT: tightly coupled, user-managed, custom build-to-order stacks with manual operations; evergreen IT loosens this coupling for better utilization and return on investment.
Example: using idle UG/PG lab machines outside class hours for researchers' simulations.
Client-server: still widely used (FTP, telnet, HTTP) but the move is towards loosely coupled, service oriented architecture.
