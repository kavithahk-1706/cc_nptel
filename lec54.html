Lecture 54 MED priority

Sustainable Cloud Computing — I

Prof. Soumya Kanti Ghosh · Dept. of Computer Science and Engineering, IIT Kharagpur

Introduces the sustainability challenges of large-scale Cloud Data Centers (CDCs) — energy consumption, carbon footprint, and the fundamental trade-off between reliability and energy efficiency. Covers the four-component conceptual model for sustainable cloud computing and key energy management techniques including DVFS. Vocabulary-heavy; know the components and the core trade-off for exams.

1 · Why Sustainable Cloud Computing?
Context: Cloud Service Providers (CSPs) — Amazon, Google, IBM, Microsoft Azure — run large, geographically distributed Cloud Data Centers (CDCs) to deliver IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS. User demand is ever-increasing and highly variable. CDCs must be available 24×7 for reliable service.
Reliability Goal
CDCs must maintain Service Level Agreements (SLAs) with users at all times. Components — servers, network, storage, memory, cooling, power supply — must operate continuously. SLA violations trigger penalties for the CSP. Spiky or correlated peak demand must be absorbed without failure.
Sustainability Goal
CDCs consume enormous energy. Energy consumption projected to reach 8,000 terawatt-hours by 2030 (near-zero in 2014). CSPs must minimize energy consumption, carbon footprint, and greenhouse gas emissions while keeping operational costs under control and return on investment viable.
Core tension: Financial and carbon footprint costs of running large CDCs negatively impact sustainability. These two goals — reliability and energy efficiency — are in direct conflict. Sustainable cloud computing seeks the best trade-off between them.
2 · The Energy Challenge in Cloud Data Centers
Problem
Idle Energy Consumption
Resources (servers, network, storage, memory, cooling) consume energy even when idle. You cannot simply switch them off — wake-up time would violate SLAs on sudden demand. Even in a home or office, systems idle 50%+ of the time while still drawing power. CDCs face this at massive scale.
Problem
Cooling Overhead
Dense packing of servers and network components generates enormous heat. Cooling systems add a second layer of energy consumption on top of the processing energy. Poorly cooled CDCs risk hardware failure and performance degradation.
Problem
Underloading and Overloading
Underloaded servers waste energy (on but not working). Overloaded servers may violate SLAs. Neither extreme is acceptable. Effective load balancing is needed to keep utilization in the efficient range — but load balancing itself adds complexity.
Solution direction
Renewable Energy Sources
CSPs (Google, Amazon, Azure, IBM) are moving toward powering CDCs with renewable energy (solar, wind) to reduce carbon footprint. Hybrid approach is common: part renewable, part grid (fossil fuel). Future CDCs must minimize greenhouse gas emissions and heat release.

CDC Relocation Strategies for Sustainability

Near waste heat recovery opportunities Near accessible renewable energy resources Near cooling stations (minimize duct length) Near power stations (minimize transmission loss) High installation overhead Must avoid same seismic zone / power grid
3 · Sustainable Cloud Computing Conceptual Model
Conceptual model: A layered architecture that offers holistic management of cloud computing resources to make cloud computing more energy efficient and sustainable. Four major components work together.
Component 1
Cloud Architecture
The standard cloud service layers: IaaS, PaaS, SaaS. The core cloud model that must be sustained. Workloads and virtual machines run at this layer; sustainability strategies are applied on top of it.
Component 2
Remote CDCs
Data centers at remote geographic locations. Virtual machines and workloads can be migrated to remote servers to balance load effectively. When a parent server is overloaded, workloads are shifted to idle remote servers — reducing energy concentration and overloading.
Component 3
Cooling Manager
Monitors thermal status of the CDC. Issues thermal alerts when temperature exceeds threshold values. A heat controller then takes action (adjusting cooling) with minimal impact on CDC performance. Dense server packing makes cooling management critical — inadequate cooling risks failure.
Component 4
Power Manager
Controls power sourcing from renewable energy (solar, wind) and from the grid (fossil fuel electricity). An Automatic Transfer Switch (ATS) manages switching between sources. Power Distribution Units (PDUs) transfer electricity to servers and cooling devices. For deadline-critical workloads, grid energy is used for reliability; for flexible workloads, renewable sources are preferred.
4 · Energy Management Techniques and Challenges
Technique
DVFS — Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling
Reduces energy consumption by scaling processor voltage and frequency up or down based on workload. Effective for reducing energy when load is low. Challenge: Switching between high and low states increases response time and service delay — can adversely affect SLA compliance.
Technique
Sleep Mode / Server Consolidation
Putting lightly-loaded servers to sleep reduces energy consumption. Challenge: Wake-up time on sudden demand can violate SLAs. Simultaneous peak from many users during wake-up = double failure. Not straightforward — requires deep analysis of demand patterns.
Challenge
Reliability Degradation from Power Modulation
Repeatedly turning resources on and off degrades hardware reliability — particularly storage devices. Excessive power cycling (turning off/on to save energy) reduces component lifespan, counteracting the sustainability goal.
Goal
Energy-Aware Resource Management
The primary objective: reduce power consumption without affecting reliability. A CSP cannot afford poor power management, but also cannot violate SLAs. Fine-grained, per-resource energy management (targeting idle or underloaded specific resources) is more effective than coarse global strategies.
5 · Key Dimensions of Sustainable Cloud Computing
Dimension
Application Model
How workloads are structured and submitted. Application-level characteristics (deadline sensitivity, parallelism) determine which energy management strategies can be applied without SLA violation.
Dimension
VM / Virtualization
Efficient VM placement, migration, and consolidation are central to reducing energy waste from underloaded servers. Secure VM migration is needed when moving workloads between CDCs for energy balancing.
Dimension
Waste Heat Utilization
Large CDCs generate significant heat. This waste heat can be recovered and repurposed (e.g., to heat nearby buildings). Relocating CDCs near waste heat recovery infrastructure is one strategy to improve overall energy efficiency.
Dimension
Thermal-Aware Scheduling
Scheduling jobs with awareness of server temperature distribution. Concentrating load on fewer servers vs. distributing across many — each has different heat and energy profiles. Optimal scheduling minimizes heat concentration while meeting workload deadlines.
Dimension
Renewable Energy
Shifting CDCs to solar, wind, and other green energy sources. Deadline-flexible workloads can be scheduled to run when renewable energy is available. Deadline-critical workloads fall back to grid power for reliability.
Dimension
Capacity Planning
Planning CDC size, server count, and server provisioning based on projected user demand growth. Over-provisioning wastes energy and capital; under-provisioning violates SLAs. Capacity planning must anticipate demand evolution and accommodate it sustainably.
Summary
Sustainable Cloud Computing: A paradigm focused on minimizing energy consumption and carbon footprint of CDCs while maintaining SLA reliability. Energy consumption of CDCs projected at 8,000 TWh by 2030 — making sustainability critical.
Core trade-off: Reliability (SLA maintenance) vs. Energy efficiency (low consumption, carbon footprint, renewable energy). Every energy-saving strategy risks some reliability loss; the goal is the best feasible balance.
Conceptual model — 4 components: Cloud Architecture (IaaS/PaaS/SaaS), Remote CDCs (VM migration for load balancing), Cooling Manager (thermal alerts + heat control), Power Manager (ATS + PDU for renewable/grid switching).
DVFS: Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling — reduces energy by scaling CPU resources, but increases response time and may violate SLAs. Example of the reliability-efficiency trade-off.
Energy-aware resource management: The primary goal — reduce power consumption without affecting reliability. Fine-grained per-resource management (idle server, idle storage) is more effective than coarse global strategies. Avoids both underloading waste and overloading SLA violations.
Renewable energy strategy: Deadline-critical workloads → grid power (for reliability). Deadline-flexible workloads → renewable energy (solar, wind). Hybrid approach allows CDCs to maximize green energy use without compromising SLAs.
